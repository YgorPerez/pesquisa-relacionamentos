      Emojis are increasingly used in the workplace, yet their impact on professional interactions remains unclear. This study examined how using positive and negative emojis in simulated workplace instant messages (IMs) influenced perceptions of the sender’s competence and communication appropriateness. In the main experimental study, participants (N = 243; 134 men, 109 women) read IMs varying in sentence valence (positive, negative, neutral), emoji valence (positive, negative, no emoji), and sender gender (man, woman). The emotional valence and realism of the text sentences were validated in a pilot study. We found that both sentence and emoji valence significantly influenced perceptions of sender competence and appropriateness in professional communications. Positive emojis enhanced competence perceptions when paired with positive or neutral sentences, but had no effect with negative sentences. Negative emojis generally decreased perceived competence, especially when paired with positive or neutral sentences. Regarding appropriateness, sentences without emojis or those paired with positive emojis were viewed as most appropriate, while those paired with negative emojis were seen as least appropriate. Interactions involving sender and recipient gender revealed that, compared to men, women judged negative messages from women (but not men) senders as less appropriate (but not less competent). These findings highlight the importance of considering emoji valence, message content, and gender in professional interactions, and demonstrate how emojis can potentially be leveraged to create a positive impression and avoid breaking social norms.

      Topics Social Psychology
      Keywords: emojis, workplace communication, technology-mediated interaction, gender, emotion

      In face-to-face interactions, unspoken cues such as facial expressions and tone of voice are essential for conveying intent and meaning. These nonverbal cues are governed by display rules, which dictate the appropriateness of emotional expression in different social contexts (Johnson et al., 1975; Moran et al., 2013; Rafaeli & Sutton, 1987). In professional settings, adhering to display rules is crucial for fostering positive workplace relationships and achieving career goals (Diefendorff et al., 2006). However, the increasing prevalence of digital communication in the workplace, particularly through text-based platforms like email and instant messaging (IM), presents new challenges for understanding and adhering to these rules. Emojis have emerged as a potential solution, serving as digital substitutes for nonverbal cues to express feelings, convey tone, and clarify meaning (Boutet et al., 2021; Erle et al., 2022; Hand et al., 2022; Herring & Dainas, 2020; Riordan, 2017). Emojis also influence social perceptions of the sender; for instance, those who use the smiling emoji are perceived as warmer, and those who use the angry emoji are perceived as more dominant (Aretz & Mierke, 2019; Boutet et al., 2021; Courtice et al., 2025; Hand et al., 2022). Therefore, emojis have the potential to enhance text-based interactions, where nonverbal expressions of emotions are not readily available (e.g., Boutet et al., 2021; Erle et al., 2022; Hand et al., 2022; Herring & Dainas, 2020; Riordan, 2017). However, the impact of emojis on professional interactions remains unclear. In the current study, we investigated the extent to which emoji use influences people’s perceptions of competence and professional appropriateness in a hypothetical professional setting. We aim to provide practical knowledge for navigating digital professional interactions and theoretical insights into the evolving landscape of nonverbal communication in work environments.

      Technology-Mediated Interactions in Professional Settings

      The rapid shift towards virtual work environments has increased reliance on text-based communication platforms, such as email and IM, for professional interactions. Indeed, IM has become a dominant form of workplace communication, with recent statistics indicating that 91% of enterprises use two or more chat platforms (Mio, 2024). Major platforms like Slack report over 42 million daily active users spending an average of 90 minutes actively working on the platform each day (DemandSage, 2025), while Microsoft Teams and other workplace messaging apps continue to show substantial growth. Furthermore, Paenrata (2023) examined IM usage patterns and sentiments reported on X (formery Twitter) and found that, since the COVID-19 pandemic, workplace IM has increased in recent years (i.e., post-2020). Although these platforms facilitate efficient communication, they lack the rich and spontaneous nonverbal cues that are present in face-to-face (FtF) interactions, such as facial expressions, tone of voice, and body language. This absence of nonverbal cues can lead to misunderstandings and misinterpretations, hindering effective communication and collaboration. Social Information Processing (SIP) theory (Walther, 1992) offers a framework for understanding how individuals navigate these complexities in digital communication. SIP theory posits that users adapt to the lack of nonverbal cues in text-based interactions by relying on other available cues to convey social information and achieve their interpersonal goals (Markus, 1987; Walther, 1992, 2011; Walther et al., 2015). Emojis have emerged as a potential solution to this challenge, offering a unique form of paralanguage that can convey emotions and clarify meaning in text-based messages (Bai et al., 2019; Erle et al., 2022; Stein, 2023). Although prior studies have documented the impact of emojis in various contexts (e.g., informal communication, social media, romantic relationships/sexual relationships; e.g., Groggel, 2023; Li & Wang, 2023; Thomson et al., 2018), there is little empirical research that explicitly addresses the impact of emojis on perceptions of competence in workplace IM communications. In the current study, we aimed to address this gap by examining the effects of emoji use on perceptions of competence and appropriateness in a hypothetical professional setting.

      Perceptions of Competence and Appropriateness

      Because display rules govern how emotions are expressed and perceived in the workplace, one might expect that emojis – which are often used to communicate or clarify emotions – are also governed by display rules. However, research on the nuances of emoji use in professional technology-mediated interactions is limited. Thus, an important question is whether using emojis in professional IMs influences perceptions of the sender’s competence, a critical dimension of social perception that is particularly relevant in workplace interactions (Cuddy et al., 2008). Results of studies that have examined this question have been mixed. For instance, Glickson et al. (2018) found that in professional contexts, smiling emoticons decrease perceptions of competence. Similarly, studies examining academic contexts found that instructors who use emojis in technology-mediated interactions with students are perceived as less competent compared to those who do not use emojis (Vareberg et al., 2023; Vareberg & Westermann, 2020). However, other studies examining professional contexts have found no impact of emojis on competence ratings of message senders (Ernst & Huschens, 2019).

      Studies examining competence may have produced inconsistent results because the influence of emojis varies depends on contextual factors such as perceived appropriateness, the relationship between communicators, and the type of emoji used (see Cheshin, 2020 for a review). For instance, people across diverse backgrounds generally share a similar understanding of appropriate emotional expression in various daily life situations, often favouring moderate levels of expressivity in professional settings (Eaves & Leathers, 2017; Stein, 2023). Furthermore, the appropriateness of specific emotional displays can vary depending on whether the setting is personal or professional (Cheshin, 2020). Expressing anger might be seen as detrimental in personal relationships, potentially harming social standing and psychological well-being. Yet, displaying anger in a professional setting may be viewed as an appropriate commitment to high standards, a display of leadership assertiveness, or a way to mobilize and motivate team members toward goal achievement (Cheshin, 2020). Some have argued that this consensus extends to the use of emojis in professional communications, where emojis that convey moderately positive emotions, such as the smiling face or thumbs-up, would be seen as more appropriate than those expressing negative emotions like the angry face or frowning emoji (Aretz & Mierke, 2019; Cheshin, 2020; Ernst & Huschens, 2019). Thus, whether emojis are viewed as appropriate likely depends on established social display rules. Moreover, emojis perceived as appropriate according to display rules are likely to enhance social perceptions of competence, suggesting that not only the choice but also the context of emoji use plays a critical role in shaping professional interactions.

      The few studies that have examined the extent to which emoji use in professional settings is considered ‘appropriate’ (i.e., in line with the norms of professional communication) have yielded conflicting results. Some studies suggest that emojis can have a detrimental impact on the perceptions of other workers, such that those who use emojis are perceived as communicating less appropriately (Aretz & Mierke, 2019; Butterworth et al., 2019; Glikson et al., 2018; Glikson & Riordan, 2024; Marder et al., 2020; Riordan & Glikson, 2020). In a recent study, researchers found that when participants perceived emoji use as more appropriate (i.e., perceived as adhering to display rules), they reported higher perceived closeness with team members and greater motivation to join the team (Glikson & Riordan, 2024). Conversely, when emoji use was perceived as less appropriate, closeness and motivation to join the team decreased. This supports the notion that it might not be the use of emojis per se that influences social perceptions in professional settings, but rather the extent to which the use of emojis are perceived appropriate (i.e., adhering to social display rules). Given the importance of display rules in human interactions and the paucity of studies that have examined how emojis influence appropriateness judgements, we examined how emojis influenced perceptions of sender competence and appropriateness in this study.

      Gender and Perceptions of Emotional Displays

      The perception of emotional displays in professional settings is influenced by the gender of both the sender and receiver due to societal expectations and gender role stereotypes that shape communication styles and interpretations (Brescoll, 2016; Brescoll & Uhlmann, 2008; Eagly et al., 1992; Eagly & Karau, 2002; Fischbach et al., 2015; Hareli et al., 2013; Marshburn et al., 2020). These stereotypes can create a double bind for women in the workplace, who may be perceived as less likable when assertive and less competent when warm (Eagly & Karau, 2002; Heilman, 2001). This bias extends to emotional expression, where women may be penalized for deviating from expected gender role norms, whether by being perceived as overly emotional or too dispassionate (Brescoll, 2016). Research on FtF interactions supports the notion that a message receiver’s gender influences how emotions are perceived in professional settings. For example, Fischer et al. (2013) found that men who cry are perceived as less competent than women who cry, and that women generally rate crying individuals more positively than men. Additionally, a meta-analysis by Thompson and Voyer (2014) found that women have a slight advantage in recognizing negative emotions, suggesting potential gender-based differences in interpreting emotional cues. Aljasir (2024) found that the gender of message recipients may also play a role in assessing the appropriateness of emojis, and that women, but not men, rated emojis as more appropriate overall when senders were men (relative to women). However, this study only had participants rate the appropriateness of the emoji itself – emojis were not attached to messages, nor was the evaluation given within a specific context (e.g., workplace settings).

      The impact of the message sender’s gender on perceptions of emoji use in professional settings has also been explored, albeit to a limited extent. Butterworth et al. (2019) found that messages containing affectionate emojis were judged as more appropriate and the sender as more likable when they came from women than from men. However, texts with less affectionate but friendly emojis were judged as equally appropriate, but the sender as more likable, when they came from men compared to from women. Similarly, Riordan and Glikson (2020) also found gender differences in perceptions of emoji use by leaders, with men perceiving emoji use as indicative of a more effective sender, while women perceived emoji use as less effective. However, these studies did not specifically examine the intersection of gender with the use of positive and negative facial emojis in IM professional contexts. Given the potential for gender to influence perceptions at both the sending and receiving end of emotional cues in digital communication, the current study also examines how sender and participant gender impact perceptions of IM-style workplace messages containing positive, negative, and neutral emojis.

      Although previous research has examined various gendered outcomes related to workplace emotional expression, including likability (Riordan & Glikson, 2020) and warmth (Eagly & Karau, 2002), we specifically focused on competence and appropriateness for several theoretical and practical reasons. First, competence represents a fundamental dimension of social perception that directly impacts career advancement and professional success (Fiske et al., 2007). Indeed, research on workplace communication effectiveness consistently highlights competence as a primary dimension of professional perception (Cuddy et al., 2008; Fiske et al., 2007). Furthermore, in professional contexts, competence perceptions predict hiring decisions, performance evaluations, and career advancement opportunities more strongly than warmth or likability (Heilman & Okimoto, 2007).

      Second, appropriateness captures adherence to professional norms and expectations, which is particularly relevant when examining novel communication practices like emoji use in professional settings. Given that emoji use represents a relatively new phenomenon in professional communication, understanding whether it violates norms of appropriate workplace behavior is essential for developing evidence-based workplace communication guidelines and training. Moreover, competence and appropriateness represent distinct but complementary aspects of professional perception. Warmth and likability primarily address interpersonal rapport; however, competence and appropriateness more directly capture professional effectiveness and norm adherence – key concerns when evaluating whether emoji use enhances or undermines workplace communication. Therefore, in the current study we prioritized examining competence and appropriateness as outcomes most relevant to understanding the gendered and professional implications of emoji use in professional communication.

      The Current Study

      In the current study, we examined how the use of positive, negative, and neutral emojis influence perceptions of competence and appropriateness of men and women message senders in a hypothetical professional setting. We selected these two outcomes (competence and appropriateness) based on their central importance to professional success and workplace communication effectiveness, especially for emerging technology-mediated communication practices. Competence perceptions directly impact career trajectories and professional credibility (Fiske et al., 2007), and appropriateness captures whether communication behaviors align with professional norms. Although other studies have examined gendered outcomes related to likability (Riordan & Glikson, 2020) and warmth (Eagly & Karau, 2002), we focused on competence and appropriateness as they most directly address the practical question of whether emoji use enhances or undermines professional image in professional contexts.

      Women and men were shown a variety of short text sentences that were either positive, negative, or neutral in valence. The emotional valence and realism of the text sentences were validated in a pilot study. In the experimental study, some participants were informed that the message sender was a woman, and others were told that the sender was a man. Text sentences were paired with emojis in either congruent (e.g., positive sentence-positive emoji) or incongruent (e.g., positive sentence-negative emoji) conditions. Other sentences were shown without emojis. For each text-emoji pairing, participants rated the emotional tone of the message, as well as their perceptions of the message sender’s competence and appropriateness (i.e., adherence to social norms). This design allowed us to examine the effects of emoji presence, emoji and sentence valence, and sender and participant gender. Based on prior research, we predicted the following:

      Hypothesis 1 (H1): Messages including negative emojis will lead to lower perceptions of competence compared to messages without emojis or with positive emojis.

      Hypothesis 2 (H2): Messages including negative emojis will lead to lower perceptions of appropriateness compared to messages without emojis or with positive emojis.

      Because research on the impact of gender in relation to IM-style professional messages with positive and negative facial emojis is scant, we included the following research questions:

      Research Question 1 (RQ1): Will women and men differ in their ratings of competence based on the emotional valence of the message, and will emoji presence have an impact?

      Research Question 2 (RQ2): Will women and men differ in their ratings of appropriateness based on the emotional valence of the message, and will emoji presence have an impact?

      Research Question 3 (RQ3): Will the gender of the message sender influence participants’ ratings of competence based on the emotional valence of the message, and will emoji presence have an impact?

      Research Question 4 (RQ4): Will the gender of the message sender influence participants’ ratings of appropriateness based on the emotional valence of the message, and will emoji presence have an impact?

      Methodology

      Participants

      A total of 404 participants completed online surveys for this study. Of these, 262 were recruited from an undergraduate pool at a large, bilingual university in Canada and 142 were undergraduate students recruited from Prolific. We excluded 118 participants from the analyses based on exclusion criteria linked to data quality for experiments conducted online (Douglas et al., 2023) and manipulation checks. Specifically, we excluded those who reported that they had a dyslexia diagnosis (n = 6), were not wearing prescribed corrective lenses (n = 1) and were flagged as outliers in terms of study duration (n = 9). We also excluded 55 participants whose survey responses indicated they were not paying adequate attention to the survey, either because they failed at least three of five reading comprehension questions (n = 19) or failed both directive questions (e.g., “Select the response negative three”; n = 37). Because gender was a key component for analyses assessing the impact of participant gender on perceptions of message senders, we excluded 14 participants who did not provide this information. For the same reason, we excluded 3 participants who reported a gender identity other than “man” or “woman” (e.g., agender, nonbinary)1. The survey for this study was only offered in English, but our undergraduate pool represented bilingual people; therefore, we excluded an additional 32 participants who reported that their preferred language was not English. We made this decision to ensure sample homogeneity and avoid potential confounds related to cross-linguistic differences in emoji interpretation and workplace communication norms. Some research suggests that emoji interpretation can vary across linguistic and cultural groups (Barbieri et al., 2016), and workplace communication norms may differ between francophone and anglophone professional contexts in Canada. Although all participants were functionally bilingual (as evidenced by their ability to complete the English survey), their preferred language likely shaped their primary professional networks and communication practices. Finally, to ensure that participants were conscious of the gender of the sender throughout the study trials, we included pre- and post-test questions asking participants to recall and report the ‘gender’ of the message sender in the scenarios they read. Forty of the remaining participants failed these manipulation checks, and were thus excluded from analyses.

      In total, we retained 243 participants for analyses in the current study. The total required sample size estimated with G-power (version 3.1; Faul et al., 2007) for F-test repeated measures, within-between interaction, five measurements, a power of 0.90 (1 - β error probability), and a small effect size of 0.2 was 60. We also conducted a post-hoc power analysis using G-power for our retained sample using the same parameters; the achieved power (1 - β error probability) was 1.00, indicating our sample (N = 243) substantially exceeded the threshold necessary to detect a small effect size.

      Of the 243 participants retained for analyses, 120 (49.4%) were from Prolific and 123 (50.6%) were from the undergraduate participant pool. Regardless of data source, all participants were undergraduate students at the time of data collection. We maintained the combined sample for our primary analyses to enhance the generalizability of our findings beyond traditional psychology undergraduate samples, while acknowledging that both groups represent the same target population of undergraduate students2. This approach allowed us to examine digital communication perceptions across a more diverse participant base while maintaining sufficient statistical power (Novielli et al., 2025). Sociodemographic information for the full sample is presented in Table 1. Our sample was included 134 (55.1%) men and 109 (44.9%) women. On average, participants had received 2.75 years of university-level education (SD = 2.18; range = 0 – 18 years) and were 21.63 years of age (SD = 2.17; range = 17 – 34). Although we did not directly measure participants’ past work experience, national statistics suggest that a substantial proportion of our sample likely had workplace experience. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2024), 44.3% of college students were employed in October 2023, with 39.6% of full-time students and 77.7% of part-time students working while enrolled. In Canada, approximately 40.8% of full-time students aged 15-24 work while studying (i.e., excluding summer months), with employment rates reaching 61% for returning students during summer months (Statistics Canada, 2024). Given that our sample included undergraduate students from both an undergraduate pool and Prolific, we can reasonably assume variation in workplace experience across our sample.

      Study Materials

      Text Stimuli

      The text sentences used as stimuli for this study were selected in a pilot validation study. A broader set of 140 sentences was created by the research team. In the first phase of the pilot, these sentences were rated by three independent raters on emotional valence and realism in a professional context. From this initial item pool, we removed sentences that were rated as unrealistic by two or more raters. Valence was determined based on agreement of two or more raters; those with no agreement were also removed to prevent ambiguity. This resulted in a total of 109 sentences being retained. Of these, those with the highest level of agreement within each valence category were selected: 20 negative, 20 neutral, and 20 positive.

      In the second phase of the pilot, participants (N = 28) rated the emotional valence and the realism of the 60 sentences with the highest level of agreement in the first phase of the pilot. The study was conducted online through Qualtrics and sentences were presented to participants in a random order. Emotional valence was measured on a nine-point Likert scale from -4 (negative) to +4 (positive), with 0 being neutral. Realism was measured on a 9-point Likert scale from -4 (unrealistic) to +4 (realistic), with 0 being neither realistic or unrealistic. We used these ratings to select sentences with comparable realism and intensity for each valence category (i.e., negative, neutral, and positive). As a result of this process, 15 negative, 15 neutral, and 15 positive sentences were chosen. There was no difference in the intensity of the negative vs. positive sentences [t(28) = .98, p = .34]. Moreover, the average ratings of the 15 neutral sentences was not significantly different from the true neutral point of 0 [t(14) = 1.41, p = .18]. A one-way ANOVA comparing realism scores for these 15 positive, 15 negative, and 15 neutral sentences showed no significant difference across these three valence categories [F(2, 42) = 2.41, p = .102]. Therefore, the pilot study confirmed that the sentences used in the present study were comparable in terms of realism, that they were interpreted according to the intended valence manipulation, and that positive and negative sentences were comparable in intensity.       Emoji Stimuli

      The text of each message was either positive, negative, or neutral in valence and was paired with either a positive emoji (i.e., 😀; “grinning face”), negative emoji (i.e., 😠; “angry face”), or no emoji. We deliberately selected these emojis to represent positive and negative emotional valence because these simple and unambiguous facial expressions minimize interpretive variability, representing clear positive and negative emotions (Boutet et al., 2021). These emojis have also been extensively validated in prior research as representing unambiguous positive and negative valences (Boutet et al., 2021, 2023; Hand et al., 2022; Jaeger et al., 2019). By limiting our selection to two clearly valenced emojis, we could systematically examine congruence and incongruence effects between message valence and emoji valence without introducing confounds from emoji complexity or ambiguity. All emojis were from iOS. The valence and intensity of the text messages and emojis were validated in a previous study with a sample (university students) and recruitment methods similar to the current study (see Boutet et al., 2021).

      Procedure

      Participants completed this study online (hosted on QualtricsTM). Upon accessing the study, participants were given an Information and Consent form. To avoid participant bias, the Information and Consent form did not include details about research goals related to gender stereotypes (this information was provided in the Debriefing Form, described below). After reviewing the Information and Consent form, participants were asked to indicate their consent to participate via button press. Participants then completed sociodemographic questions.

      Next, participants began the experimental phase of the study. First, participants read task instructions outlining the experimental procedure, which included questions to ensure they understood the task they would complete in this study phase. Specifically, participants read: “In the next part of the experiment, a series of text messages will appear on the screen. Please be sure to read the text messages carefully, because you will be asked to answer a series of questions following each one. These texts are being sent to you by a [male/female] co-worker in a professional setting. Assume that this is a [male/female] co-worker with whom you frequently work on the same projects. Keep this in mind when answering the questions.” This instruction established several key contextual elements: (1) the messages came from a co-worker, establishing a peer-level professional relationship, (2) this was someone with whom the participant frequently collaborated, suggesting an established working relationship, and (3) the setting was explicitly professional. The combination of “co-worker” and “frequently work on the same projects” was designed to evoke a familiar, collaborative professional relationship without specifying a particular industry or hierarchical dynamic. Depending on the condition participants were randomly assigned to, the instructions indicated that the messages participants would read were ‘sent’ by either a (i) male (man sender condition) or (ii) female (woman sender condition) co-worker. We chose to manipulate sender gender through explicit gender labels (a “male” or “female”) to eliminate potential confounds associated with specific names, such as perceived age, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status that can be inadvertently signaled through name selection (Bertrand & Mullainathan, 2004). This method aligns with our focus on gender as a social category rather than individual identity.

      The effectiveness of this manipulation, as well as the professional context, was confirmed through our manipulation checks. On a new page, participants were asked who would be sending them the messages (a male or a female) and in what context (professional or romantic), the correct answers being: a male in the context of a professional relationship (for the man sender condition) or a female in the context of a professional relationship (for the woman sender condition). The fact that participants correctly identified the professional relationship context in the manipulation check (rather than selecting romantic relationship options) provides some evidence that the professional framing was salient. Of the 283 participants who passed all other exclusion criteria, 243 (85.9%) correctly recalled the sender’s gender and the professional context both immediately after the instructions and at the end of the study. This high retention rate indicates that our explicit gender labeling was salient and memorable throughout the experimental task, validating our methodological approach.

      The stimuli for the experimental phase of the study were 45 text-emoji pairings; specifically, negative, neutral, or positive texts paired with either a negative, positive, or no emoji. We created 12 different text-emoji pairing permutations and randomly assigned participants to one of them. After exposure to each text-emoji pairing, participants rated: (i) the emotional valence of the message (-3 to 3: negative to positive, with 0 being neutral), (ii) their impression of the message sender’s competence (-3 to 3: very incompetent to very competent, with 0 being neutral) and, (iii) the degree to which they felt the sender was violating workplace norms (-3 to 3: very norm-violating to not at all norm-violating, with 0 being neutral).

      After completing the experimental phase of this study, participants were asked to take a two-minute break. After the break, participants completed the following measures: the Media and Technology Usage and Attitudes Scale (MTUAS; Rosen et al., 2013), the Ten Item Personality Inventory (TIPI; Gosling et al., 2003)3, and the Ambiguous Sexism Inventory Shortened Version (ASI; Glick & Whitehead, 2010)4, as well as questions regarding whether they were distracted while completing the study and, if so, and what the distraction was. As a final manipulation check, participants were again asked who had sent them the messages, and in what context. Finally, participants were presented with a Debriefing Form, which explained the goals of the study and revealed the Sender Gender manipulation.

      Results

      Analytic Approach

      For all analyses, we conducted 3 x 3 x 2 x 2 mixed Analyses of Variance (ANOVAs), with Sentence Valence (negative, neutral, positive) and Emoji Valence (negative, no emoji, positive) as repeated-measures variables, and Sender Gender (man, woman) and Participant Gender (man, woman) as independent-groups variables. Preliminary analyses revealed that data source (undergraduate pool vs. Prolific) showed significant interactions with emoji valence for competence and appropriateness ratings, and with sentence valence for emotional tone ratings. Prolific participants were on average 5 years older (M = 24.18 vs. 19.15) and had approximately 2 more years of higher education. However, we retained the combined sample for several reasons: (1) both samples represent young adults likely to use digital communication in workplace settings; (2) combining samples increases generalizability beyond a single university population; and (3) controlling for data source as a covariate did not alter our substantive conclusions (see Supplemental Materials).

      We tested the assumption of sphericity with Mauchly’s Test of Sphericity in SPSS. In all analyses, the assumption of sphericity was violated; therefore, our results report the corrected Greenhouse-Geiser degrees of freedom. For all pairwise t-tests, we adjusted the alpha level to p = .010. We followed up significant interactions with post-hoc t-tests to examine the nature of interaction effects.       Discussion

      The current study investigated how the use of emojis in workplace IMs influence perceptions of the sender’s competence and the appropriateness of the communication. Men and women read IM-style messages that varied in sentence valence, emoji valence, and sender gender, and then rated the sender on these dimensions. Results revealed that the effects of emojis on social perceptions are nuanced and depend on both the valence of the emoji and the valence of the text message, with some small effects of gender of the message sender or receiver.

      Emoji Valence and Congruency Effects

      Consistent with Hypothesis 1, negative emojis were associated with lower perceptions of competence, especially when paired with a neutral or positive sentence. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that negative emotional displays can harm perceptions of competence in professional settings (Aretz & Mierke, 2019; Butterworth et al., 2019; Glikson et al., 2018). These findings also align with the broader literature on display rules, suggesting that people are expected to regulate their emotions in professional settings to maintain a positive image and avoid negative repercussions (Johnson et al., 1975; Moran et al., 2013; Rafaeli & Sutton, 1987). The effect of positive emojis on perceived competence depended on the valence of the text message. When paired with positive or neutral sentences, positive emojis enhanced perceptions of competence relative to negative emojis, but not relative to no emojis. However, when paired with negative sentences, positive emojis decreased perceived competence relative to both negative and no emojis. This finding suggests that perceptions of competence related to use of positive emojis in professional communication is contingent on the overall tone of the message. When the positive emoji establishes or reinforces the positive tone (e.g., a positive emoji with a neutral or positive message), it can maintain positive perceptions of the sender’s competence. However, when the positive emoji is incongruently paired with a negative message, it may create a sense of dissonance or insincerity, leading to negative evaluations of the sender. Indeed, we found that negative emojis paired with a negative sentence yielded higher perceptions of competence compared to when negative emojis were paired with an incongruent positive or neutral sentence. This suggests that messages that are incongruent and ambiguous are seen as an indicator of incompetence. Still, negative emojis produced low perceptions of competence overall, even when paired with a congruent negative sentence. This suggests that using the negative emoji has a negative impact on perceptions of competence.

      The findings for perceived appropriateness were partially consistent with Hypothesis 2 and the findings for competence, such that negative emojis were associated with lower perceptions of sender appropriateness overall. For positive emojis, the effect on perceived appropriateness depended on the valence of the text message: when paired with positive or neutral messages, positive emojis enhanced perceptions of appropriateness relative to negative emojis, but when paired with a negative sentence, positive emojis did not decrease perceptions of appropriateness. However, positive sentences were not perceived as more appropriate when paired with a positive emoji compared to when no emoji was present, suggesting that perceptions of appropriateness are mainly driven by the overall tone of the message rather than a particular emoji. Our findings build on research in FtF interactions by demonstrating that even in text-based interactions, communications that violate display rules by expressing inappropriate emotions (especially negative ones) are perceived as inappropriate (Johnson et al., 1975).

      Past research has suggested that people perceive emoji use in workplace settings as inappropriate, while mixed results have been reported for competence ratings (Aretz & Mierke, 2019; Butterworth et al., 2019; Ernst & Huschens, 2019; Glikson et al., 2018; Glikson & Riordan, 2024; Marder et al., 2020; Riordan & Glikson, 2020; Vareberg et al., 2022; Vareberg & Westerman, 2020). Our findings suggest that the logical next step for this body of research is to explore how the interaction between emoji valence and message content shapes workplace communication outcomes. Specifically, future research should examine how emoji-text congruence or incongruence affects not only perceptions of the sender (as we studied here) but also subsequent communication behaviors, relationship development, and collaborative outcomes. For instance, does receiving incongruent emoji-text combinations (e.g., positive text with negative emoji) influence how recipients craft their own responses? Do such mixed signals affect willingness to collaborate on future projects or trust in professional relationships? Future research should therefore adopt more holistic approaches that consider how emojis function within broader communication patterns, examining outcomes such as conversation flow, rapport building, conflict resolution, and team cohesion in digital workplace environments.

      Impacts of Sender and Receiver Gender

      Based on past research (Aljasir, 2024; Butterworth et al., 2019; Riordan & Glikson, 2020), we also explored the influence of sender and participant gender on perceptions of competence or appropriateness. We found that sender gender (but not participant gender) impacted perceptions of emotional tone. This finding is consistent with the notion that emojis that convey agreeableness are judged as more appropriate and likable when sent by women compared to men (Butterworth et al., 2019); however, the effect size for this finding was very small. Thus, it is possible that gendered perceptions of emotions have an effect that is somewhat muted in technology-mediated interactions. No research (to our knowledge) has directly assessed the impact of emojis on perceptions of men’s and women’s workplace competence, but in FtF workplace settings, women tend to be judged as less competent when expressing negative emotions (Brescoll, 2016; Brescoll & Uhlmann, 2008; Eagly et al., 1992; Eagly & Karau, 2002; Fischbach et al., 2015; Hareli et al., 2013; Marshburn et al., 2020). It is only possible to speculate why we did not observe large effects of sender gender similar to those reported in the literature. One possibility is that technology-mediated communication reduces gender salience in workplace communications, thereby mitigating the detrimental impacts of negative emotional expression on perceived competence for women. Another possibility is that our sender gender manipulation was not sufficiently salient to replicate real-world interactions with a known other whose gender is an integral part of their identity. Indeed, we excluded a small subset of participants (N = 40) who failed to correctly respond to manipulation check questions that asked them to identify the gender of the message sender in our experimental paradigm. Finally, the specific emojis used in our study may not have strongly activated gender stereotypes. Butterworth et al. (2019) reported that the kissing-face emoji, which was deemed more appropriate when sent by women, was also considered more agreeable than a positive emoji similar to the one used in this study. Therefore, gender effects may be more pronounced when using emojis that unambiguously convey affection. Future research should use more explicit gender cues (e.g., with a traditionally gendered display name and/or picture accompanying each message) to more fully explore the potential role of gender in emoji interpretation.

      We also found that, relative to men participants, women participants perceived women senders as less appropriate when a negative emoji was present. Although this effect was small, it does align with past research. In the FtF literature, Gabriel et al. (2018) found that women report experiencing more incivility from other women than from men, with this effect being amplified for women who are more agentic (e.g., assertive, expressive of negative emotions) at work. In our study, women may have judged women more negatively for violating gendered expectations of ‘appropriate’ emotional expression for women in the workplace (Eagly, 2009). The Participant Gender × Sender Gender interaction observed in this study suggests that re-evaluating some previously reported findings with sender gender considered as a factor may offer new insights into the dynamics of gendered communication in digital interactions. For instance, Riordan and Glikson (2020) found gender differences in perceptions of emoji use by leaders, with men perceiving emoji use as indicative of a more likable and effective sender, while women perceived emoji use as less effective. Similarly, Aljasir’s (2024) found that women, but not men, rated emojis as more appropriate overall when senders were men (relative to women). Our findings extend this past research, suggesting that women’s perception of negative emoji use may vary depending on whether the sender is a woman or a man. Indeed, future research should account for multiple gender dynamics (i.e., sender and receiver gender) as well as subtleties in emoji use (e.g., emoji valence) when examining these and other related research questions.

      Emojis as Nonverbal Cues in Professional Communication

      Our findings contribute to the growing body of research on the role of emojis as nonverbal cues in digital communication. The Panksepp-Jakobson hypothesis (Jacobs, 2015; Panksepp, 1998) suggests that emojis may tap into our shared evolutionary heritage of facial expressions, eliciting similar neurological responses as real-world nonverbal cues. This could explain why emojis are so effective in conveying emotional information and influencing social perceptions, even in the absence of traditional nonverbal cues like facial expressions and tone of voice. In FtF interactions, receivers integrate information conveyed by verbal and nonvverbal channels to assign meaning to interactions. While the relative importance of these two channels is still debated, it has been argued that in FtF communication, signals conveyed by nonvverbal cues drive the interpretation of verbal messages (e.g., Guerrero & Floyd, 2006). While there is evidence that emojis can intensify, reverse, and disambiguate text messages (Boutet et al., 2021; Filik et al., 2016; Hand et al., 2022), research on emojis in technology-mediated interactions remains in its early stages. Much remains to be learned about the relative weight of signals conveyed by text versus emojis, particularly in virtual work environments, where text often serves as the primary mode of communication.

      Practical Implications for Workplace Communication

      Our findings have several practical implications for individuals and workplaces. First, they highlight the importance of being mindful about the potential impact of emojis on professional interactions. Emojis are not simply neutral add-ons to text messages; they can influence how others perceive us, particularly in terms of competence and appropriateness. Positive emojis can enhance perceptions of competence when used appropriately, but they should be used cautiously when the message conveys negative information. Negative emojis should generally be avoided, as they can decrease perceptions of competence regardless of the message content. Second, our findings underscore the importance of aligning the emotional tone of the emoji with the content of the message. Incongruent text-emoji pairings may create confusion and lead to negative perceptions of the sender, potentially undermining the intended message and damaging professional relationships. By understanding the nuances of emoji use, professionals can leverage these digital tools to enhance their communication and build stronger workplace relationships. However, caution is warranted, as overusing emojis can undermine their effectiveness (Stein, 2023) and potentially harm one’s professional image.

      Limitations and Future Directions

      The current study is not without limitations. First, our manipulation of sender gender – introduced in a single sentence at the beginning of the study – may not have sufficiently mimicked real-world interactions with a known other whose gender is an integral part of their identity. This limitation may have reduced our ability to detect effects of sender gender. Furthermore, we used explicit gender labels to avoid name-based confounds; however, we acknowledge that this approach may lack the subtlety of real-world gender cues. Future research could compare different gender manipulation methods (e.g., names, pronouns, explicit labels) to examine whether results are consistent across operationalizations. Second, the study used a hypothetical IM scenario. We focused on IM as our communication context in the current study because it represents a highly relevant form of contemporary workplace communication (Paerata, 2023). IM’s unique position between formal and informal communication, combined with its synchronous nature and expectation of quick responses, creates an ideal context for examining how professionals negotiate emoji use in real-world digital communication settings. However, the IM context may not fully capture the complexities of real-world workplace communication. Similarly, in the current study we used emojis that previous research has validated as expressing positive and negative emotional valence. Although our use of validated stimuli is a strength of our design, we acknowledge that workplace communication involves a broader range of emojis. Future research should examine a wider range of emojis and their use in more naturalistic settings to determine whether the observed effects generalize to actual workplace interactions. Third, our sample consisted primarily of young adults from Canada, who may have limited workplace experience. Future research should include more diverse samples to explore potential differences based on work experience, age, and cultural backgrounds. Our sample also combined university students and Prolific participants who differed in age and educational attainment, which is a common difference among these sampling sources (Novielli et al., 2025). Although including data source as a covariate in all analyses revealed that the pattern of results remained mostly consistent, accounting for these demographic differences still influenced the magnitude of effects (see Supplemental Material). Future research should examine whether emoji perception effects vary systematically across career stages and professional experience levels. Finally, there is evidence that interpersonal closeness can influence emotion communication in text-based interactions (Derks et al., 2008). In the current study, the relationship between the fictional sender and the study participant is, by design, superficial. For instance, we did not specify the hierarchical relationship or familiarity level between message senders and receivers. This ambiguity allows for broader generalization of our findings; however, it prevents us from examining how power dynamics or relationship history might moderate emoji effects. Although there is little research examining these effects, some findings indicate that hierarchical relationships influence digital communication interpretation (Glikson et al., 2018), and perceptions of emoji appropriateness likely varies between peer-to-peer versus supervisor-subordinate communications (Kemp & Clayton, 2017). Future research should systematically vary relationship contexts, examining how emoji effects differ when messages come from supervisors versus peers, or from familiar versus unfamiliar colleagues. Such investigations would provide more nuanced guidance for emoji use in specific workplace relationships. By addressing these questions, researchers can develop a more comprehensive understanding of how emojis can be used effectively in the workplace to enhance communication and foster positive relationships.

      Conclusion

      The current study provides novel insights into the role that emojis play in shaping perceptions of competence and appropriateness in professional settings. The findings highlight the importance of considering both emoji valence and message context when using emojis in the workplace. While positive emojis can enhance perceptions of competence when used with neutral or congruent messages, negative emojis should generally be avoided. We also found that women participants perceived negative emojis sent by women senders more negatively than those sent by men. These findings highlight the complexities of virtual work and text-based interactions, and the need for continued research into the variables that shape these dynamics. Our study contributes to a deeper understanding of the intricate interplay between verbal and nonverbal cues in technology-mediated communication and provides practical insights for navigating the evolving dynamics of workplace interactions.
