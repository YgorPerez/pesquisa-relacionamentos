Abstract

This article addresses conversational dynamics in interpersonal influence situations. Drawing on interaction ritual theories and the research of interaction processes and patterns, we argue that sequential transition patterns of task and social–emotional acts can capture essences of conversational interaction ritual.A successful ritual then generates emergent solidarity and induces desired outcomes of interpersonal influence. In two experimental situations of online text chats between strangers (advice-giving, Nconversation = 336 with a student sample and compliance-gaining, Nconversation = 737 with a MTurk sample), we found certain transition patterns consistently predicted influence outcomes and interpersonal perceptions. Aligning with our theoretical expectations, these transitions indicate the moment-to-moment emergence of joint attention and shared emotion, which are essential ingredients of a successful interaction ritual.

Interpersonal influence, broadly defined as the process of behavioral change (including psychological antecedents) through interpersonal communication, covers a variety of communication phenomena including asking favors, giving advice, obtaining permission, persuasion, and among others (Dillard & Wilson, 2014). Communication researchers have advanced understandings of interpersonal influence through unpacking the strategies, goals, and other aspects of message production by the source of influence, as well as the effects of influence messages and techniques on the target (see Dillard et al., 2002; Wilson, 2002; also Cialdini & Sagarin, 2005). However, interaction and relational dynamics between the source and target are often less spotlighted, despite their importance in understanding interpersonal influence (Dillard et al., 2002; Wilson, 2002) and, in general, theorizing the roles that interpersonal communication plays in our social lives (Cappella, 1987; Solomon et al., 2021).

This study addresses the interaction and relational dynamics of interpersonal influence through an interaction ritual perspective. Interaction ritual refers to the micro-social processes that generate solidarity, moment by moment, in social encounters. Such rituals pervade in everyday life and, if successful, can transmute self-contained individuals into an emergent collective (Goffman, 1967). This transmutation is particularly achieved through rhythmic entrainment of interactions that engage joint attention to the cause of gathering and shared emotion among individuals (Collins, 1981, 2004). Conversations (including mediated ones, Ling, 2008) are the typical embodiment of interaction rituals (Collins, 1981), making the solidarity-generating micro-social processes germane to interpersonal influence situations. We thus propose that a successful episode of interpersonal influence is likely an emergent, solidary encounter, and influence outcomes should be associated with conversational dynamics that achieve joint attention and shared emotion, moment by moment (or “incrementally and interactively,” Sanders & Fitch, 2001, p. 263).

It is not a new idea that interpersonal influence may rely on communication that induces a sense of solidarity (e.g., acts of politeness, Baxter, 1984; Craig et al., 1986; Lim, 1990; Lim & Bowers, 1991; and the cooperativeness and agreeableness emerged in conversations, Sanders & Fitch, 2001). The issue is that related studies often hinge on discourse principles or strategies that are grounded in specific linguistic forms and conventions (e.g., the linguistic strategies of politeness, Brown & Levinson, 1978), which can be challenging to identify and generalize in empirical data (Craig et al., 1986). At the same time, previous work also tends to be context-specific and depends on qualitative observations, which can make global theories or generalizable knowledge claims difficult (Dillard et al., 2002; Wilson, 2002).

As detailed later, we tackle the issue through a marriage of three lines of work: First, interaction ritual theories (Collins, 1981, 2004; Goffman, 1967) provide a micro-sociological/social psychological conceptualization of the interaction and relational dynamics that can lead to solidarity-induced influence in interpersonal influence situations. Second, communication research on interaction patterns (e.g., Burgoon et al., 1995; Cappella, 1980; Giles & Smith, 1979) suggests that certain quantifiable conversational dynamics can be indicators and constituents of the micro-social processes in conversational interaction rituals. Finally, the distinction between task and social–emotional acts by Bales (1950) and other social theorists (e.g., Benne & Sheats, 1948; Deutsch, 1949; Parsons & Bales, 1955) help to pinpoint the specific conversational dynamics that contribute to a successful interaction ritual and thereby influence outcomes. Bales’ methods also guide our operationalization of such dynamics as sequential transitions between task and social–emotional acts across conversational turns.

In short, we expect interpersonal influence to be predicted by the extent of sequential transitions that signal and constitute the codevelopment of joint attention and shared emotion in conversations. It is noteworthy that these sequential transitions differ from previously studied sequential influence strategies (e.g., the “foot-in-the-door” and “door-in-the-face” techniques for persuasion, Dillard et al., 1984; and the sequence of supportive acts in advice interactions, Feng, 2009; Goldsmith, 2000), or the source-to-target linguistic accommodation for compliance-gaining (Giles et al., 2007). Whereas these other temporal phenomena are one-sided and/or operate as a macro-structure of an encounter, our focus is the moment-to-moment micro-dynamics within an encounter. Also, because interaction ritual and solidarity are, in essence, collective phenomena, both source-to-target and target-to-source transitions are treated equally. At the same time, if solidarity is the mechanism that links interaction ritual and interpersonal influence, we should to some extent observe comparable influence outcomes from both the source and target, despite their role differentiation (Liao et al., 2021).

Below, we first briefly review the discourse research on interpersonal influence as a precursor of the current inquiry. Next, we discuss interaction ritual and emergent solidarity as a meaningful avenue to interpersonal influence and elaborate on how such rituals may be associated with conversational dynamics, especially in terms of sequential transitions of task and social–emotional acts. We then present a quantitative study of two influence situations––compliance gaining and advice giving, with independently collected text conversations between strangers from two online experiments. The analyses based on simple Markov models (Cappella, 1980; Kaplan, 2008) are reported, and the implications are discussed.

The discourse of interpersonal influence

So far, the most relevant research on interaction and relational dynamics in interpersonal influence is a relatively small body of discourse research (for detailed reviews, see Dillard et al., 2002; Wilson, 2002). Early work in this literature attempted to understand interpersonal influence through general principles of discourse (e.g., the conversational maxims: talk clearly, succinctly, relevantly, and truthfully, per Grice, 1975; or specific constitutive rules of speech acts such as making a request per Searle, 1969; see Wilson, 2002). The most notable example is the investigations of Brown and Levinson’s (1978) politeness theory, which stipulates that people strive to minimize threats to face (i.e., self-image defined per socially approved attributes in a given situation, Goffman, 1956) while maintaining autonomy through linguistic strategies. Some of these strategies are found prevalent in interpersonal influence situations (e.g., Baxter, 1984; Craig et al., 1986). Later work started to examine sequential and temporal features of politeness strategies and other sociolinguistic strategies during a conversation (e.g., de Turck, 1985; Fitch, 1994; Goldsmith, 2000), leading to the acknowledgement of interpersonal influence as necessarily an interactive and incremental process over time (Sanders & Fitch, 2001).

Deriving generalized conclusions from these studies can be challenging, however (Dillard et al., 2002; Wilson, 2002). The discourse approaches tend to prioritize context-specificity and ethnomethodological description over global theorization (Cicourel, 1980). Their frameworks also tend to be grounded in linguistic forms or conventions (Wilson, 2002), which lack clear connections to concrete social and social–psychological mechanisms. For example, Brown and Levinson’s (1978) politeness strategies (e.g., notice, attend to the hearer; exaggerate interest, approval, sympathy with the hearer; use in-group identity markers; seek agreement; assert common ground; joke; etc.) may cover many social–psychological mechanisms, yet these linguistic strategies per se are hard to generalize without further theorization (Craig et al., 1986).

Nonetheless, this early discourse research reveals that certain social forces (e.g., the urge to be polite or maintain face) govern conversational regularities in interpersonal influence situations. They also suggest a causal role of communication, such that the conversationalists, through speech acts, incrementally and interactively develop cooperativeness and agreeableness as emergent relational outcomes, which then foster interpersonal influence (Sanders & Fitch, 2001). These insights prompt us to attempt a more social–psychological theorization of the interaction and relational dynamics of interpersonal influence, with quantitative observations.

Interaction rituals, emergent solidarity, and interpersonal influence

The starting point of our inquiry is Goffman’s (1967) concept of interaction ritual. His earlier work on self-presentation (1956) argues that people are driven to maintain positive self-images, or face, in terms of socially approved attributes. This endeavor is achieved through engaging in symbolic interactions of facework (Goffman, 1967). Whereas Brown and Levinson’s (1978) politeness theory elaborates on the idea of facework with individual’s strategic, communicative behaviors, Goffman’s (1967) own theorization is intersubjective. Particularly, Goffman (1967) argues that facework, when sets in motions as sequences of equilibrial, interchanging acts between two or more participants (p. 20), constitutes the everyday rituals through which people are mobilized as self-regulating participants in preserving the social order of societies, “if they are to be societies” (p. 45). In other words, interaction rituals channel human prosociality into the moment-to-moment creation and maintenance of social order, or solidarity––which is a sacred end in itself beyond the specific causes of given encounters (Durkheim, 1912); and conversations are a typical embodiment of such rituals (Collins, 1981; Goffman, 1967).

Our argument, then, is twofold: First, emergent solidarity is a causal factor in interpersonal influence situations. Second, if solidarity emerges from interaction ritual, then certain conversational dynamics can lead to solidarity-induced influence. For example, a person may buy Girl Scout cookies after an engaging and pleasant chat that makes the person feel, even just for a moment, identified with the girls and their purposes, despite the lack of personal need, existent interdependence, or chance of future encounters. In other words, the influence outcomes (e.g., buying) become ritualistic and detached from instrumental concerns, partly or fully.

The role of solidarity in interpersonal influence situations has been touched by the aforementioned discourse research (e.g., Lim & Bowers, 1991; Sanders & Fitch, 2001), and the relevance of politeness in such situations (Baxter, 1984; Craig et al., 1986) may also be interpreted as people’s tendency to build and maintain solidarity. Conceptually, the nature of such solidarity-induced influence is more akin to conformity due to group processes (see Levine & Tindale, 2015) than compliance with personal requests as conventionally studied by interpersonal communication researchers (Dillard et al., 2002; Wilson, 2002). This is because the emergent solidarity in effect invokes a sense of common reality that resembles a group (Collins, 1981), which then exerts normative influence on the conversationalists in accordance to the specialized goal of such a temporary group. The target in an interpersonal influence situation would comply with a request, if the request was ritualistically legitimized as the ideal or norm of the emergent group. It is noteworthy that although interpersonal influence research typically focuses on the target’s behavioral change, the source of influence may also exhibit comparable changes because the emergent solidarity should apply to all ritual participants (Liao et al., 2021).

That said, the above solidarity-induced influence differs from conformity in formal groups. Solidarity-induced influence requires a genuine commitment to the momentary coalition as there is no formal structure to enforce conformity. Moreover, the solidarity-induced influence is not guaranteed, because the emergent solidarity from an interaction ritual is an ongoing construction: The extent of solidarity depends on the success of the ritual; and in conversational interaction rituals, conversational dynamics are the mechanism to such a success (Collins, 1981).

What conversational dynamics, then, constitute a successful ritual and lead to the solidarity-induced influence? Goffman (1967) suggests the sequence of conversational acts must be in “equilibrium” (p. 16) and made into “accommodative lines” (p. 42), although these imageries and Goffman’s ethnomethodological analyses may be difficult to generalize beyond specific types of encounters. Collins (1981, 2004) offers a more generalizable, micro-sociological/social–psychological theory: A successful interaction ritual is made of rhythmic entrainment of interactions that engage (a) joint attention to the common cause of gathering and (b) shared emotion across individuals (also “common cognitive reality” and “common emotional tone,” Collins, 1981, p. 999; or “mutual focus of attention” and “shared mood,” Collins, 2004, p. 48).

Joint attention and shared emotion are the most basic ingredients and intermediate results at any point of a successful ritual (Collins, 1981; see Collins, 2004 for other ingredients and outcomes for broader group phenomena). Joint attention signifies the common reality and indicates the specific “cultural resources” (Collins, 1981, p. 999)––things that are considered acceptable for the membership of the momentary coalition, including the desired behavioral changes in interpersonal influence situations. At the same time, shared emotion constitutes the motivational, “emotional energies” (Collins, 1981, p. 1001, also Collins, 2004, p. 102) that sustain the joint attention and gradually develop into a feeling of solidarity. A minimal level of positive emotion is needed as the universal tone of solidarity (Collins, 1981, 2004), although specific negative emotions can also play roles depending on contexts (e.g., sadness for in-group social support and anger toward out-groups for in-group cohesion).

According to Collins (1981, 2004), joint attention and shared emotion feed into each other as a mutually intensifying feedback loop in the generation of solidarity, and thus are needed together for a successful ritual. At the same time, joint attention and shared emotion are rooted in the prosocial behavioral tendencies of human communication (i.e., the tendency to attend together to the same thing for cooperation, Tomasello, 1995; the tendency to mimic and share emotions, Hatfield et al., 1993; Rimé et al., 1992). They thus should be manifested in communicative acts such as talking about a common topic and expressing similar emotions. Accordingly, the feedback loop likely takes forms of rhythmic entrainment of interactions, such as synchronized verbal and nonverbal features (Collins, 2004). This makes related conversational dynamics at heart of the rituals that are responsible for solidarity-induced influence.

In sum, we can now reiterate our proposition: A successful episode of interpersonal influence is likely an emergent, solidary encounter, and its influence outcomes should be associated with certain conversational dynamics that build and maintain joint attention and shared emotion, moment by moment. The remaining task is to identify these conversational dynamics for quantitative analyses (as the sociological studies of interaction rituals tend to also rely on ethnomethodology, e.g., Collins, 2004; Goffman, 1967; Ling, 2008).

Sequential transitions of task and social–emotional acts as ritualistic interactions

A candidate for quantifying conversational dynamics for interaction rituals is the sequential transition patterns across conversational turns. These patterns often take the form of reciprocity or complementarity, that is, the transition between acts with similar or complementary/contrasting functional meanings (e.g., matching each other’s smiles or compensating other’s gaze with gaze-aversion, see Burgoon et al., 1995 for a further classification). They are prevalent in human interactions (Cappella, 1981; Chartrand & Lakin, 2013) and related to various biological, psychological, and social needs and functions (Burgoon et al., 1995).

More importantly, communication scholars have particularly noticed the social-constructive nature of these patterns: Giles and colleagues (e.g., Giles & Smith, 1979; also see Giles, 2016) argue that people negotiate their relationship with other individuals or social groups through the convergence (and divergence) of speech features, as a form of accommodation (and nonaccommodation). This resonates with Goffman’s idea of “accommodative lines,” which are the basic means that organize interaction rituals (1967, p. 42); and studies have found communication accommodation can lead to emergent rapport and compliance (see Soliz & Giles, 2014; also Liao et al., 2021). Similarly, Burgoon et al. (1995) argue that interaction patterns are in essence an on-going process of adaptation between personal (biological and psychological) predispositions and social expectations. These ideas suggest that certain sequential transition patterns may signal and even constitute the entrained interactions for conversational interaction rituals.

That said, the mere form (e.g., reciprocity and complementarity) of sequential transition (as often focused by the related communication and psychology research) is insufficient to capture the codevelopment of joint attention and shared emotion in interaction rituals. Beyond its form, functional meanings of the transitioning communicative acts need to be substantiated theoretically. To this end, the distinction between task and social–emotional acts (Bales, 1950) is particularly useful: Bales (1950) observed that interactions among members of an interacting group can almost always be behaviorally categorized into: (a) attending to the group task and get things done (e.g., ask and give orientation, opinion, and suggestion) or (b) maintaining a positive feeling towards each other (e.g., show solidarity, tension release, and acceptance; and avoid the opposite, e.g., antagonism, tension, and rejection). Bales and other theorists (e.g., Benne & Sheats, 1948; Deutsch, 1949; Parsons & Bales, 1955) concurred in this distinction as it reveals two basic functions of human interaction––(a) to collaborate on given tasks and (b) to maintain prosocial relationships––which universally apply to various cooperative social units, including but not limited to small groups. Similar distinctions are also discovered in intragroup and interpersonal conflicts, which are in essence failed cooperation due to malfunctioning tasks and/or relationships (Barki & Hartwick, 2004; Jehn, 1997).

Task and social–emotional acts are likely the micro-elements of joint attention and shared emotion in interaction rituals, and they are also relevant in interpersonal influence situations. A ritual is essentially a collaboration brought by a given cause of gathering, and thus the joint attention must comprise interactants’ task acts that attend to the cause. In interpersonal influence situations, people do not engage in such typical tasks as problem-solving in small groups. However, they must maintain a shared focus on the cause of the gathering (e.g., favor, advice, or sales), as any deviation would signal objection and undermine the situations (Clark et al., 1994).

Similarly, shared emotion can be created through social–emotional acts with the same emotional tone. The relevance of politeness to interpersonal influence (Baxter, 1984; Craig et al., 1986) also indicates the ubiquity of positive social–emotional acts in such situations. Although it is possible to further distinguish between positive and negative social–emotional acts (Bales, 1950), we focus on the positive acts for three reasons: First, as mentioned before, a positive tone is universally required for the feeling of solidarity and negative emotions are not––and their functions are context-dependent and require differentiating discrete emotions (e.g., sadness, anger, anxiety, and fear). Second, negative social–emotional acts are infrequent in short-term, zero-history groups (Bales, 1950; Keyton & Beck, 2009), which resemble many interpersonal influence situations. Negative social–emotional acts are also likely suppressed in these situations due to the prevalence of politeness. In fact, such negative acts were very infrequent in our data reported later (less than 0.2%; see Table 1). Finally, as revealed below, adding negative social–emotional acts (and differentiating negative emotions) can exponentially complicate the theoretical analyses with a relatively small gain of context-specific predictions.

With the functional distinction between task and social–emotional acts (positive), we can now operationalize the conversational dynamics for joint attention and shared emotion as sequential transition patterns involving these two types of acts. First, because a conversational turn can contain multiple acts, it can be classified into at least three states, involving task act only (T), social–emotional act only (E), or both (TE). Given the arguably essential status of the distinction between task versus social–emotional acts in many social interactions (Parsons & Bales, 1955), a turn that cannot be classified is expected to be rare and functionally ignorable. Other turn-level details such as the relative weights and sequential order between task and social–emotional acts (i.e., TE or ET) are also conceptually less important and thus ignored.

Then, there can be nine types of transition between two adjacent turns: T–T, T–E, E–T, E–E, T–TE, TE–T, E–TE, TE–E, and TE–TE. Because joint attention and shared emotion can be manifested in the synchronization of acts (Collins, 2004, p. 75), we may identify joint attention or shared emotion, separately, at a given moment by tracking the reciprocity of task or social–emotional acts. For example, a momentary joint attention is indicated in four transitions where task acts are reciprocated (i.e., T–T, T–TE, TE–T, and TE–TE), and the other five transitions suggest the task (i.e., cause of gathering) is unsolicited (E–T and E–TE), unreciprocated (T–E and TE–E), or not mentioned (E–E). Similarly, four transitions suggest shared emotion is momentarily achieved (i.e., E–E, TE–E, E–TE, and TE–TE), whereas in the other five transitions, a social–emotional act is unsolicited (T–E and T–TE), unreciprocated (E–T and TE–T), or never present (T–T).

The separate reciprocity of task acts or social–emotional acts already suggest the nine transitions differ in functional values for interaction ritual. However, a successful ritual requires joint attention and shared emotion together. Thus, we need to jointly analyze the two types of reciprocity. Although such a joint analysis challenges a linear ranking across the nine transitions, we can at least distinguish between a less-functional set and a functional set.

Less-functional and functional transitions for interaction ritual and influence outcomes

Out of the nine transitions, there are six less-functional ones in three pairs (a) with only unreciprocated acts (T–E and E–T), (b) with one and only one type of reciprocated acts (T–T and E–E), or (c) with an unreciprocated act despite the reciprocity of the other type of act (TE–T and TE–E). These transitions all deviate from the ideal rhythmic entrainment of interactions as discussed so far. Specifically, T–E and E–T show the least amount of entrainment due to the lack of reciprocity, which may signal objections (Clark et al., 1994) or psychological differentiation (i.e., nonaccommodation, Giles, 1978). There is also no clear reason to expect that responding to a task act with a social–emotional act, or vice versa, is complementary for cooperation or solidarity-building. Comparably, T–T and E–E indicate separate reciprocities of task and social–emotional acts, which are insufficient, however. The mutually intensifying feedback loop between joint attention and shared emotion is a refined coordination or rhythmic entrainment (Collins, 2004). Such entrainment is unlikely to be achieved in rigid, turn-by-turn manners, for example, having joint attention at one turn and shared emotion at another. Finally, TE–T and TE–E are the most coordinated transitions so far, as they involve reciprocity of either task (TE–T) or social–emotional act (TE–E), in addition to an attempt to initiating the other type of act. However, this attempt was explicitly not reciprocated and thus signal psychological differentiation (Giles, 1978) at the moment and the delicate coordination of interaction ritual may be sensitive to such small hiccups (Collins, 2004).

In contrast, the remaining three transitions (TE–TE, E–TE, and T–TE) are likely functional for interaction rituals, with some nuanced differences. First of all, TE–TE indicates a momentary achievement of both joint attention and shared emotion, as task and social–emotional acts are both present and reciprocated. It thus can be an indicator of rhythmic entrainment of interaction in a ritual. There is, however, a noteworthy complication, especially in the early stage of an encounter. According to Collins (2004), the rhythmic entrainment of joint attention and shared emotion takes time to codevelop (Collins, 2004), and thus one may not expect to observe TE–TE early on. Yet, the prevalence of politeness in interpersonal influence situations (Baxter, 1984; Craig et al., 1986) suggests another possibility: people may pretend to be engaging and friendly and thus apparently exhibit TE–TE at least at the beginning of an encounter to comply with social expectations. With this caveat in mind, we still expect TE–TE to be, overall, an indicator of a functional ritual, because the fake politeness is unlikely to last long in a ritual and the presence of politeness is still context dependent (Craig et al., 1986).

For the above reason, T–TE and E–TE may be more dynamic signals of a functional ritual. In these transitions, either joint attention or shared emotion is momentarily achieved, while the other ritual ingredient is emerging as an unsolicited social–emotional or task act: for example, a task act is reciprocated (i.e., joint attention) while a (positive) social–emotional act is added (T–TE), or a (positive) social–emotional act is reciprocated (i.e., shared emotion) while the task is reminded (E–TE). Although joint attention and shared emotion are not yet achieved together at the moment, the emerging trends embodied in T–TE and E–TE may be more realistically indicate the codevelopment of joint attention and shared emotion in their mutually intensifying feedback loop. Accordingly, two of the aforementioned less-functional transitions, TE–E and TE–T, may also be considered as dynamic signals, but of a failing feedback loop.

In sum, we expect the three functional transitions to contribute to solidarity-induced influence and thereby desired interpersonal influence outcomes. For these outcomes, we focus on the target’s perceived effectiveness of the conversation and context-defined behavioral outcomes, which are typical in persuasion and interpersonal influence research (Cappella, 2018). In contrast, the six less-functional transitions should be either detrimental or ineffective on these outcomes. We do not assert these less-functional transitions are all detrimental, as current theories (Collins, 1981, 2004; Goffman, 1967) do not have a clear answer on whether a failed ritual would negatively impact solidarity-induced outcomes or simply have no effect. That said, should a less-functional transition have no negative effect, we expect it to be at least ineffective with an effect size smaller than a benchmark of trivial effect (since the absence of effect cannot be directly tested). We also do not expect all the three functional transitions or the six less-functional transitions to have same effects on the two influence outcomes, given the complexity of a conversation as a dynamic process. Therefore, we postulate two hypotheses below.

H1. Target’s perceived effectiveness and behavioral outcome (adherence to advice or compliance with request) are positively associated with the three functional transitions (TE–TE, T–TE, and E–TE).

H2. Target’s perceived effectiveness and behavioral outcome (adherence to advice or compliance with request) are trivially, if not negatively associated with the six less-functional transitions (TE–T, TE–E, T–T, E–E, T–E, and E–T).

To further explore the interaction ritual perspective for interpersonal influence, we also investigate if comparable outcomes of the source are also associated with these functional and less-functional transitions, as the emergent solidarity should apply to all ritual participants. That said, the source–target role differentiation may complicate the effects, and we thus ask a research question for simplicity.

RQ1. How are the source’s outcomes (perceived effectiveness and behavioral outcome) associated with the functional and less-functional transitions?

In addition, we also examine whether the source and target form positive perceptions of each other in relation to these functional transitions, as that would be expected should solidarity emerge from an interaction ritual (Goffman, 1967). Same as we mentioned before, it is less clear whether the less-functional transitions would have no impact or negative impacts on the perceptions. Therefore, we ask the research question below regarding perceived warmth and competence, which are universal dimensions of social cognitions (Fiske et al., 2002).

RQ2. How are the source and target’s interpersonal perceptions of each other (warmth and competence) associated with the functional and less-functional transitions?

A note on mediated communication and interaction ritual

Our discussion so far does not distinguish between face-to-face and mediated communication, although our study reported below relies on online text conversations. It is necessary to point out that mediated communication can impose constraints on interaction rituals. For example, joint attention may be interrupted as people simultaneously engage in multiple mediated encounters. Individuals’ ability to convey emotions (e.g., through nonverbal means) may also be hindered, thus changing how shared emotion is achieved. Nevertheless, mediated communication researchers generally agree that people can adapt their communicative practices for social relationships in mediated environments (Walther, 1996). Technological features of mediated conversations, such as emoticons and “language play” (e.g., playfully organized text replies), also offer alternative forms of emotional communication (Herring, 1999).

Studies have suggested that the logic of interaction ritual applies to mediated environments (e.g., online forum and chatroom, DiMaggio et al., 2018; Liao et al., 2021; and mobile texting, Ling, 2008). Although certain rituals would benefit from “bodily copresence” (e.g., religious ceremonies, live concerts, sports games; possibly as an evolutionary relic of humans’ attuned mind to physical presence, see Collins, 2004, p. 53), the necessity of face-to-face communication becomes increasingly an open question in the face of evolving technologies (Collins, 2020). The bottom line is: whereas communicative acts can differ in forms (e.g., facial expressions vs. emoticons), interaction rituals would still require rhythmic entrainment of interactions for joint attention and shared emotion. Our operationalization of such interactions as sequential transitions between task and social–emotional acts may also be adapted to incorporate channel-specific features. That said, we study mediated, text conversations, because we believe it is the proliferation of such conversations to date (by mobile technologies and social media) that renewed the opportunity to examine conversations for interpersonal influence and interpersonal communication phenomena in general.

Methods

We studied text chats from two larger projects: one examined compliance-gaining for donations to a children’s charity and the other examined advice-giving for sleep disturbance (the studies’ interfaces can be viewed in Supplementary Table S1). In both situations, participants assumed a source or target role; and those who played the source can be considered as semi-confederates, although their outcomes were also studied. Such a design has a benefit to minimize factors external to a conversation (e.g., source and target characteristics and the history of relationship), thus allowing a clearer observation of interaction ritual as an intrinsic, emergent process in zero-history encounters. We also tried to improve the ecological validity through the specific study designs as described below.

Participants and procedures

Compliance gaining for charity donation

A total of 2,984 English speaking adults (18 years or older) in the United States were recruited with a $2 compensation through mturk.com for an online conversation task ostensibly generating donations to a children’s charity. Participants were randomly assigned to be a source or a target before entering a dyadic, anonymous chat. Sources were instructed to persuade the partner to donate some or all of their compensation ($2) to the charity. Sources were provided with information about the charity and brief descriptions of potential persuasive strategies (e.g., logical, credibility, and emotional appeal), and were instructed to explicitly ask the partner to donate at the end. Meanwhile, targets were told to chat with a partner about a children’s charity. Both participants had to each take at least 10 turns (M = 10.32, SD = 0.55) before they could leave the conversation. Then, both participants were prompted, in private, to donate any amount out of their incoming compensation ($2) in a separate input box. They were led to believe the decision will impact their compensation, although they were actually paid in full and debriefed.

Participants completed a survey thereafter. After removing incomplete conversations, 737 conversations and 1,004 participants were kept (repeated participations were allowed). Their average age was 36.75 years (SD = 11.64). They were 53.59% female, and 73.90% Caucasians, 7.77% Asians, 7.87% African American, 5.88% Hispanic, and 1.79% Native American.

Advice giving for sleep disturbance

A total of 948 participants (18 years or older, able to speak and read English, and residing in the United States) were recruited from undergraduate students at a West Coast University in the United States. The participants were compensated with course credits. Participants were instructed to have an online chat about sleep health. To enhance the ecological validity, we asked the participants to take a sleep quality survey before engaging in the conversational task. The survey asked questions about sleep disturbance adopted from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th edition). Participants were assigned the source role if their answers to every survey item were below the scale’s neutral point, as this indicates good sleep quality. Otherwise, they assumed the target role. In other words, the targets indeed reported having sleep disturbance issues. Then, sources were instructed to chat and give advice to a fellow participant who has sleep problems, with instructions to (a) initiate the conversation by introducing themselves as a consultant, (b) give sleep advice (randomly chosen from two versions: having a wind-down routine or not using electronic devices before sleep; see Supplementary Table S1), and (c) end the conversation by giving a link to a webpage about sleep health. Meanwhile, targets were told that they had sleep disturbance and would be conversing with a sleep consultant. Both participants were asked to each speak at least 10 turns, and were encouraged to carry out a natural, complete conversation. After the conversation, they completed a survey about the conversations and their partners. In seven days, participants completed a follow-up survey to report if they followed the advice.

After removing conversations where one or both participants left before completion, 336 conversations and 672 participants were kept for the current study. Participants’ average age was 19.94 years (SD = 2.51). They were 76.57% female, and 20.15% Caucasians, 49.10% Asians, 1.04% African Americans, 19.85% Hispanic, 0.30% Native Americans, and 9.55% Multiracial. On average, participants each spoke 10.75 turns (SD = 2.82) during their conversations.

Survey measures

All measures were taken from both source and target, and the overall descriptive statistics of scale means are reported below. See Supplementary Table S2 for the statistics of scale means separated for source and target and Supplementary Table S3 for item-level descriptive statistics.

Influence outcomes

In the compliance dataset, perceived effectiveness of compliance gaining was measured with five items on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), adapted from Zhao et al. (2011). Following the same prompt “overall, the conversation…” the items include “has a strong reason for donating to Save the Children,” “is important to me,” “puts thoughts in my mind about wanting to donate to Save the Children,” “is something that I agree with overall,” and “is convincing.” The score was 3.63 on average (SD = 0.91). Behavioral outcome was measured as the amount of donation (M = 0.30, SD = 0.52).

In the advice dataset, perceived effectiveness of advice giving was measured with four items on a 5-point semantic differential scale, following the prompt “the consultant’s responses were…” for targets or “the partner’s responses were…” for sources. The items include “ineffective–effective,” “unhelpful–helpful,” “not beneficial–beneficial,” and “inadequate–adequate” adopted from Feng and MacGeorge (2010). The average score was 3.98 (SD = 1.02). Seven days later, behavioral outcome was measured with two items: the agreement to “[i]n the past SEVEN (7) DAYS, I followed the sleep advice I was given” on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) and the answer to “[h]ow many days in the past SEVEN (7) DAYS did you follow the sleep advice,” ranging from 0 to 7 days, which were scaled to 1 to 5. The average score of these two items was 2.40 (SD = 1.25).

Interpersonal perceptions

In the compliance dataset, participants rated each other using eight adjectives on a 5-point scale (1 = not at all, 5 = very much; adopted from Fiske et al., 2002). Competence was measured with “competent,” “confident,” “efficient,” and “intelligent” (M = 3.76, SD = 1.02). Warmth was measured with “friendly,” “well-intended,” “trustworthy,” and “sincere” (M = 3.87, SD = 1.01).

In the advice dataset, participants rated each other using ten pairs of adjectives on a 5-point semantic differential scale (adopted from Wiggins, 1979). Competence was measured with “incompetent–competent,” “ignorant–knowledgeable,” “irresponsible–responsible,” “unintelligent–intelligent,” and “foolish–sensible” (M = 3.12, SD = 1.37). Warmth was measured with “uncharitable–charitable,” “cruel–tender,” “unsympathetic–sympathetic,” “coldhearted–kind,” and “distant–friendly” (M = 3.11, SD = 1.22).

Confirmatory factor analyses of survey measures

For each dataset, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the measurement quality of perceived effectiveness, behavioral outcome, warmth, and competence. The CFA models were estimated with robust statistics using the full-information maximum-likelihood estimator implemented in the R package lavaan. The model fit was judged per conventional criteria (CFI > 0.95, TLI > 0.95, SRMR < 0.08, and RMSEA < 0.05 with the acceptance of the close-fit hypothesis at ɑ = 0.05 level; see Kline, 2016, pp. 273–275). For the compliance dataset, the original CFA does not fit data, with scaled χ2(72) = 252.71 (p < .001; unscaled χ2 = 403.94), CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.98, SRMR = 0.02, RMSEA = 0.06 (90% CI = [0.05–0.06], pclose-fit = .032, rejecting the close-fit hypothesis). Similarly, the original CFA for the advice dataset also does not fit data, with χ2(98) = 345.65 (p < .001; unscaled χ2 = 440.97), CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.03, RMSEA = 0.07 (90% CI = [0.07–0.08], pclose-fit < .001, rejecting the close-fit hypothesis).

To improve the model fit, we first inspected the correlation residuals of the original CFAs for any absolute discrepancy greater than 0.10 (see Kline, 2016, p. 240; see Supplementary Table S4 for the residuals). Then, we iteratively correlated a set of within-factor item errors based on model modification indices per conventional practices (see Kline, 2016, pp. 270–271; see Supplementary Figure S1 for the diagrams of the revised models and conceptual discussions for the correlated errors). The revised models fitted data better: For the compliance dataset, the revised model’s scaled χ2(67) = 144.711 (p < .001; unscaled χ2 = 227.57), CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.02, RMSEA = 0.04 (90% CI = [0.04–0.05], and pclose-fit = .997, accepting the close-fit hypothesis). The improvement is significant, with Δχ2(5) = 90.68 (p < .001) relative to the original model. For the advice dataset, the revised model’s scaled χ2(91) = 139.92 (p = .001; unscaled χ2 = 173.53), CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.04, RMSEA = 0.04 (90% CI = [0.03–0.05], pclose-fit = .996). The improvement is also significant with Δχ2(7) = 153.82 (p < .001).

The revised models suggest reasonable measurement quality. For composite reliability, we calculated hierarchical omega (Kelley & Pornprasertmanit, 2016), which is superior to Cronbach’s alpha by allowing varying factor loadings and correlated errors. Per the revised CFA for the compliance dataset, the omegas are 0.94, 0.85, 0.97, and 0.94 for perceived effectiveness, behavioral outcome, competence, and warmth, respectively. Per the revised CFA for the advice dataset, the omegas are 0.92, 0.95, and 0.93 for perceived effectiveness, competence, and warmth (behavioral outcome was measured by a single item, the donation). Correlations among most factors are between 0.16 and 0.25 in the compliance dataset and 0.14 and 0.75 in the advice dataset (see Supplementary Figure S1), except for the correlation between warmth and competence (rcompliance = 0.93, radvice = 0.90). The latter high correlations are less of a concern, since warmth and competence are generally correlated (Fiske et al., 2002), and the current investigation treats them as both indicators of positive interpersonal perception instead of studying their differences.

Finally, we obtained factor scores from the revised CFAs for later analyses because such scores are less affected by measurement errors. Using these factor scores yielded results similar to using scale means (see Supplementary Table S10 for estimated transition probabilities and Supplementary Figure S2 for visualized transition effects, based on outcomes measured as scale means, factor scores from the revised CFAs, and factor scores from the original CFAs).

Interaction coding procedure

We trained eight research assistants to code the total 1,073 conversations from the two datasets. Drawing on Bales (1950) and other’s work (Roter & Larson, 2002), we devised a coding scheme (see Supplementary Table S5) for three categories of act: task (T), positive social emotional (E), and negative social emotional (N), with N included for comprehensiveness. Coders determined the binary existence of each category independently for every conversational turn. Coders went through three rounds of training: First, they learned the scheme and coded 10 conversations with Nturn = 210, and Krippendorff’s αs for T, E, and N were 0.57, 0.57, and 0.34. Next, they discussed the disagreement and recoded the same conversations, with the α improved to 0.83, 0.80, and 0.48 for T, E, and N. Then, the coders coded another 14 conversations (Nturn = 273), and the αs for T, E, and N were 0.80, 0.82, and 0.46. The low α for N was due to the low frequency of this category (see Table 1); and this is a feature of Krippendorff’s α (2004). Given that we focus on the T and E categories, we deemed the intercoder agreement acceptable and proceeded to coding the other conversations (N = 1,065, including conversations with low agreements during the training). The remaining conversations were randomly divided into two parts: 855 conversations were separately coded by the eight coders and 210 conversations (19.72%) were coded by all coders during the same time. For the conversations coded by all coders, the αs were 0.80, 0.76, and 0.50 for T, E, and N (Nturn = 4,352; see Supplementary Table S6 for intercoder agreements within each dataset) and the disagreements were resolved by taking the rounded average for each binary decision across all coders.

We then classified each turn as T, E, N, TE, TN, EN, or TEN (see Supplementary Table S7 for sample quotes). As expected, turns that contain N were infrequent, so were related transitions (see Table 1). In light of our argument before, we ignored N from all turns, leaving only three states of each turn (T, E, and TE) and nine possible transitions (T–T, E–E, TE–TE, T–E, E–T, T–TE, TE–T, E–TE, and TE–E; see Supplementary Table S7 for quoted examples).

Analytic strategies for sequential transitions

H1, H2, RQ1, and RQ2 essentially inquire the associations between the nine transitions and the four variables (two influence outcomes and two interpersonal perceptions). These associations were obtained in three steps. First, for each variable, we divided the dataset into successful and unsuccessful conversations based on the variable’s median (i.e., top 50% vs. bottom 50%) using the CFA-derived factor scores. A transition’s effect on each variable is therefore its probability difference between the two groups of conversations. As a robustness check for such median divisions, we also compared the top and bottom conversations using 40%, 30%, 20%, and 10% as the cutoffs, which returned similar results (see Supplementary Figure S3). Since we also concern the target and source’s outcomes separately, we divided the conversations twice of reach variable: one for source and one for target.

Then, for each group of conversations (successful or unsuccessful in terms of an outcome variable), we estimated the probabilities of the nine transitions, along with their SEs and 95% CIs (bootstrapped from 5,000 resamples, with percentile CI). This is achieved using a first-order, simple Markov model (Cappella, 1980; Kaplan, 2008). Such a model takes the turn-taking sequence of a conversation as an observation (e.g., [T][E][TE][E][E][T][E][TE]…) and stipulates that the state of a turn (i.e., T, E, or TE) was solely determined by the previous turn’s state. The model does not differentiate between source-to-target transitions and target-to-source transitions, aligned with the idea that these turns are part of the joint process of interaction ritual.

Finally, we compared the estimated transition probabilities between successful and unsuccessful conversations, using a t test (two-sided, with unpooled SE based on the bootstrapped SEs). For the three function transitions, such significance tests determine if these transitions indeed have greater probabilities among the successful conversations than the unsuccessful ones. For the six less-functional transitions, we performed additional equivalence tests (as specialized one-sided t tests, see Lakens, 2017) to determine if their nonsignificant effects also have effect sizes (Cohen’s d) that are empirically bounded within [−0.3, 0.3]. We choose 0.3 as the bound because it is a relatively small effect size and can be detected at α = 0.05 with the statistical power approaching to 80% given our smallest sample size (n = 168 in the advice dataset, which is half of the 336 conversations after the median division; see Lakens, 2017 for calculated sample sizes given the power and significance level for the equivalence test).

For all tests, α = 0.05, and we also report test results at α = 0.1 as “marginally significant.” Within each dataset, we adjusted all p values for family-wise error rate, as we do not assume all three functional transitions (or all six less-functional ones) to have effects on both influence outcomes (or both interpersonal perceptions). We used the Holm–Bonferroni method for the adjustments. Particularly, the p value of any of the three functional transition’s effect on any of the two influence outcomes (or any of the two interpersonal perceptions) was adjusted as padj = p * (6−k + 1) with k equal to the p value’s ranking (smallest first) among the six p-values of six comparisons (three transitions by two interchangeable variables). Similarly, p values for the six less-functional transitions were adjusted as padj = p * (12−k + 1) with k equal to the p value’s ranking among the 12 p-values of the 12 comparisons (six transitions by two interchangeable variables). The Markov models were estimated using the R package msm (see Supplementary Table S10 for all estimated transitions probabilities). Our data and computer codes can be found at osf.io/t87d4.

Results

Before reporting the results, the Markov models’ overall goodness-of-fit were assessed as Pearson χ2 between the observed and predicted frequencies in the 3 × 3 transition matrix (i.e., {T, E, TE} → {T, E, TE}). Across all models, χ2 ranged between 0.003 and 3.487 (see Supplementary Table S8) and were all nonsignificant at df = 6 (i.e., the number of independent cells in the transition matrix), suggesting an adequate model fit. We also assessed the stationarity assumption of these models, following Cappella (1980): Across all possible turn-to-turn transitions (i.e., turn 1–2, 2–3, …, 19–20 for every model), 70.63% have nonsignificant deviations (α = 0.01; same as Cappella, 1980) from the model-derived probabilities (see Supplementary Table S9). Most deviations were found at the beginning and the end of a conversation, which likely had different dynamics compared with the remaining turns. Considering the large sample size and turns, we consider this result acceptable.

Table 2 provides a high-level summary of all significance tests and equivalence tests (see Supplementary Table S11 for the actual statistics) and Figure 1 visualizes the tested differences. The overall pattern confirms our theoretical expectations: Two out of the three functional transitions (i.e., TE–TE and T–TE) consistently predicted both influence outcomes and positive interpersonal perceptions across the advice and compliance dataset, especially for the targets. In contrast, five out of the six less-functional transitions had either trivial effects (TE–E, E–E, T–E, and E–T) or negative effects (T–T) on these outcomes across the two datasets. The remaining less-functional transition TE–T had either trivial or negative effects on some outcomes. We specify these results below for the hypotheses and research questions.

Did functional transitions predict influence outcomes?

H1 stipulates that targets’ influence outcomes should be positively associated with the three functional transitions (TE–TE, T–TE, and E–TE). Overall, as in Table 2 (see the dark-shaded results in the top section; also Figure 1), H1 is supported fully for T–TE (4/4 effects), partly for TE–TE (2/4 effects), and marginally for E–TE (1/4 effects).

Regarding targets’ perceived effectiveness, the difference in transition probability between the top and bottom 50% advice conversations was .08 for TE–TE (SE = 0.02, padj = .005, Cohen’s d = 0.36) and .06 for T–TE (SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.56), but nonsignificant for E–TE (padj > .999). Similarly, the difference in the compliance dataset was .08 for TE–TE (SE = 0.02, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.36) and .06 for T–TE (SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.56). Such an effect was also significant for E–TE (.05 with SE = 0.02, padj = .008, Cohen’s d = 0.23).

For targets’ behavioral outcome in the advice dataset, which was measured a week later, only T–TE had a significant effect (.04 with SE = 0.01, padj = .002, Cohen’s d = 0.40), and nonsignificant for TE–TE and E–TE (both padj > .999). A similar pattern was detected in the compliance dataset for the actual amount of donation, with a significant effect of T–TE (.05 with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.40) and a marginally significant effect of TE–TE (.04, SE = 0.02, padj = .088, Cohen’s d = 0.18) but a nonsignificant effect of E–TE (padj > .999).

In general, T–TE appears to be the most consistent functional transition, followed by TE–TE and then E–TE. It is noteworthy that most of the abovementioned effects’ sizes (Cohen’s d) were above 0.3, which was used as the benchmark in the equivalence tests reported below.

Did less-functional transitions have trivial, if not negative effects on influence?

H2 speculates that the six less-functional transitions (TE–T, TE–E, T–T, E–E, T–E, and E–T) should have trivial, if not negative effects on targets’ perceived effectiveness or behavioral outcome. This was examined using equivalence tests (Lakens, 2017) in conjunction with significance tests. As Table 2 shows (see the light-shaded results; also Figure 1), H2 is supported fully for E–E (4/4 effects), largely for TE–E, T–E, and E–T (3/4 effects), and partly for TE–T (2/4 effects), regarding their effects being within ±0.3 of Cohen’s d. H2 is also supported fully for T–T (4/4 effects) and marginally for TE–T (1/4 effects) in terms of negative effects. In short, effects of all six less-functional transitions were largely expected.

Particularly, regarding targets’ perceived effectiveness of advice giving, successful conversations had a lower T–T probability (−.05 with SE = 0.01, padj = .001, Cohen’s d = −0.44), and nonsignificant differences in the probabilities of TE–T, TE–E, E–E, T–E, and E–T with min(padj) = .598, compared with unsuccessful conversations. Among these five nonsignificant differences, two transitions had significantly smaller effects than ±0.3 Cohen’s d, that is, E–E (d < 0.01, padj < .001) and E–T (d = −0.05, padj= .003); and T–E also had a marginal-significantly smaller effect size (d = −0.15, padj = .061) than ±0.3.

A similar pattern was replicated in the compliance dataset, with a lower probability of T–T (−.07 with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = −0.55) and TE–T (−.08 with SE = 0.02, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = −0.36) in successful conversations, and nonsignificantly different probabilities of TE–E, E–E, T–E, and E–T with min(padj) = .290. All of these four nonsignificant transitions had effect sizes smaller than ±0.3 (Cohen’s d = 0.05, −0.05, 0.09, and −0.16 for TE–E, E–E, T–E, and E–T) with max(padj) = .002.

A similar pattern was also found for targets’ behavioral outcome. In the advice dataset, T–T had a significant negative effect (−.04 with SE = 0.01, padj = .049, Cohen’s d = −0.32) and the other five less-functional transitions (TE–T, TE–E, E–E, T–E, and E–T) all had nonsignificant effects (all padj > .999). Among these nonsignificant effects, TE–T, TE–E, E–E, and T–E had smaller effect sizes than ±0.3, with Cohen’s d = 0.07, −0.03, 0.12, and −0.09, and padj = .005, .001, .028, and .010, respectively. E–T also had a smaller effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.16) with a marginal significance (padj = .094). This pattern was largely replicated in the compliance dataset, with a negative effect of T–T (−.05 with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = −0.41), a marginally significant negative effect of TE–T (−.05 with SE = 0.02, padj = .094, Cohen’s d = −0.20), and nonsignificant effects of all the other four transitions, with min(padj) = .673. The sizes of these marginal-significant and nonsignificant effects were also within ±0.3 (Cohen’s d = −0.20, 0.07, −0.04, 0.14, and −0.03 for TE–T, TE–E, E–E, T–E, and E–T) with max(padj) = .011.

In general, T–T stood out as antifunctional and detrimental, followed by TE–T, whereas other transitions had relatively trivial effects on targets’ influence outcomes.

Did the transitions comparably affect the sources’ outcomes?

Our RQ1 inquires if these transitions had effects on sources’ perceived effectiveness or behavioral outcome, despite their different role than the targets. As shown in Table 2 and Figure 1, the pattern is sparse but not empty. Among the three functional transitions (TE–TE, T–TE, and E–TE), E–TE had a positive effect on sources’ perceived effectiveness in the advice dataset (.10 with SE = 0.03, padj = .001, Cohen’s d = 0.42), whereas T–TE had a positive effect on sources’ perceived effectiveness in the compliance dataset (.05 with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.40). For behavioral outcomes in both datasets, only nonsignificant effects were detected for the three functional transitions, with min(padj) = .114.

Among the six-less functional transitions (TE–T, TE–E, T–T, E–E, T–E, and E–T), T–T had a negative effect on sources’ perceived effectiveness in the compliance dataset (−.05 with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = −0.40). Equivalence tests suggested effect sizes of almost all less-functional transitions (except for T–T) were within ±0.3 Cohen’s d, although effect sizes of the functional transitions (except for T–TE) were also largely within the same range (see Figure 1).

In sum, although effects of the nine transitions were sparser on sources than targets, there is evidence that some ritual mechanisms, particularly those embodied in T–TE (functional) and T–T (antifunctional), were shared between the two parties.

Did the transitions affect the source and target’s perceptions of each other?

Finally, RQ2 asks how the transitions affected interpersonal perceptions between the source and target. As revealed in Table 2 and Figure 1, the pattern generally mirrors that of influence outcomes, and thus we only report the pattern here (see Supplementary Table S10 for test statistics). Overall, two functional transitions, TE–TE and T–TE, were positively associated with targets’ competence and warmth perceptions toward the sources (4/4 comparisons for each transition; see Table 2). T–TE was also associated with the sources’ competence and warmth perceptions toward the targets (4/4 comparisons), despite its opposing effects between the two datasets. We will return to the opposing effects later.

Among the less-functional transitions, T–T was negatively associated with targets’ competence and warmth perceptions toward the sources (4/4 comparisons; see Table 2). T–T was also associated with the sources’ competence and warmth perceptions toward the targets (4/4 comparisons), despite the opposing effects between the two datasets, again. In addition, TE–T was negatively associated with targets’ competence and warmth perceptions toward the sources in the compliance dataset. The remaining four less-functional transitions (TE–E, E–E, T–E, and E–T) generally had effects smaller than ±0.3 Cohen’s d (see Table 2 and also Figure 1), suggesting their effects were trivial on the interpersonal perceptions between the source and target.

As mentioned before, it is noteworthy that T–TE and T–T’s effects on sources’ perceptions were opposite between the two datasets. Particularly, T–TE (functional) had positive effects, as expected, on sources’ perceptions of targets in the compliance dataset (.04 for competence with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.39; and .05 for warmth with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.42). However, the effects were unexpectedly negative in the advice dataset (−.05 for competence with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = −0.46; and −.08 for warmth with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = −0.69). At the same time, T–T (antifunctional) had negative effects expectedly on sources’ perceptions of targets in the compliance dataset (−.05 for competence with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = −0.40; and −.05 for warmth with SE = 0.01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = −0.42), but the effects were unexpectedly positive in the advice dataset (.05 for competence with SE = 0.01, padj = .004, Cohen’s d = 0.40; and .08 for warmth with SE = .01, padj < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.65). We discuss this pattern along with the general findings below.

Discussion

Across two interpersonal influence situations (advice giving and compliance gaining), we found certain sequential transitions between task and social–emotional acts consistently predicted influence outcomes and interpersonal perceptions (see Table 2 and Figure 1). Particularly, the outcome variables were positively predicted by the probabilities of TE–TE and T–TE transitions, which indicate a momentary achievement of joint attention together with shared emotion or a signal of its emergence. This aligns with our expectations about the solidarity-induced influence in interaction ritual, in which joint attention and shared emotion are key ingredients (Collins, 2004). In contrast, we found most of the less-functional transitions, which deviate from the codevelopment of joint attention and shared emotion, had nonsignificant and relatively trivial effects. One of these less-functional transitions, T–T (but also TE–T in one dataset), negatively impacted the outcome variables. Compared with sources, we found targets were more sensitive to the transitions’ effects––which is reasonable since the influence outcomes are congruent to their role in such situations. Yet, some of the sources’ outcomes were also affected, suggesting that, to some extent, both parties experienced the solidarity-induced influence.

Our findings have several implications for the study of interpersonal influence. First, interaction ritual theories (Collins, 1981, 2004; Goffman, 1967) offer an alternative perspective to study conversational dynamics of interpersonal influence. Compared with previous work (e.g., the study of politeness and facework; Baxter, 1984; Craig et al., 1986; Fitch, 1994; Lim, 1990; Sanders & Fitch, 2001), this perspective is more generalizable because of its roots in basic social functions of interactions (i.e., task and socioemotional interactions, Bales, 1950; Parsons & Bales, 1955) and behavioral tendencies of human prosociality (i.e., the interactional coordination for joint attention, Tomasello, 1995; and shared emotion, or sympathy, Hatfield et al., 1993; Rimé et al., 1992). A direct benefit, then, is the utilization of quantitative observations based on these functions and tendencies, in response to the call for quantitative studies of interpersonal influence discourse (Dillard et al., 2002; Wilson, 2002). At the same time, the interaction ritual perspective takes the higher-order problems of interpersonal communication (Cappella, 1987) to heart by focusing on the micro-social process at the levels of interaction pattern and its embeddedness in sociorelational structures. Scholars have also called for understanding interpersonal influence at these levels beyond the psychological accounts of message production and effect, but the progress is so far limited (Dillard et al., 2002; Wilson, 2002) even within the small body of discourse research. For example, Browns and Levinson’s (1978) politeness theory does not directly theorize interactions and is largely a motivational account of actions (i.e., people strive to maintain face). The interaction ritual perspective advances the conceptualization of the interactive and incremental process of interpersonal influence (Sanders & Fitch, 2001) as the rhythmic entrainment of interactions for joint attention and shared emotion (Collins, 1981, 2004). This conception may not characterize all interaction and relational dynamics in interpersonal influence situations, but it is likely the baseline.

Second, our theorization can be related to the study of goal and message production in interpersonal influence situations by considering interaction ritual as part of the generative mechanisms of conventional goals (Wilson, 2014) in such situations. Scholars have argued that interpersonal influence is more likely to be achieved when influence attempts are expected to be appropriate by both the source and target (Dillard & Wilson, 2014). Such normative expectations may be understood as conventional goals (in contrast to individuals’ own desires and wants, or personal goals), which are not always given but need to be constructed or negotiated as an agreement between the source and target (Wilson, 2014). Collins (2004) argues that a successful ritual would eventually lead to shared symbols of relationship and standards of morality, which likely constitute the conventional goals. In this sense, it is also possible to interpret interaction ritual as a progression from the expression of individual feelings and thoughts (expressive logic) to eventually the negotiated and coordinated symbolic understandings of social selves and situations (rhetorical logic), in O’Keefe’s (1988) terms. Although these message design logics are often thought of as individual-developmental differences (O’Keefe, 1988), their link to interaction ritual is possible––if we view the progression of these logics as a within-encounter or -relationship process through which self-contained individuals become an emergent collective with shared symbolic understandings.

Third, our findings resonate with previous studies, regarding the critical role of emotional communication in interpersonal influence situations (e.g., Feng, 2009). Our unique contribution is the emphasis on the moment-to-moment coordination of shared emotion, along with the micro-dynamic feedback loop between shared emotion and joint attention, in addition to more macroscopic sequential strategies (e.g., providing emotional support before problem inquiry and analysis in advice-giving situations; Feng, 2009). This is most evident in the negative effect of T–T (and negative or trivial effects when emotion is unreciprocated in TE–T and E–T), in contrast to the positive effects of TE–TE and T–TE. The findings not only refute the idea that one can focus on “business talks” only, but also challenge the notion that it may be okay to temporarily focus on “business talks” and deal with social–emotional talks in later turns, or vice versa. In addition, we found T–T to be the most frequent transition in both datasets (see Table 1), suggesting the moment-to-moment engagement of emotion is a persistent challenge in interpersonal influence situations. That said, shared emotion must codevelop with joint attention from the interaction ritual perspective. Talking emotionally without jointly attending to the “task” (i.e., E–E, TE–E, and to some extent, E–TE) was found infertile.

Finally, it is noteworthy that the idea of solidarity-induced influence suggests interpersonal influence can involve not only compliance (e.g., in response to a personal request or advice) but also conformity due to group processes, following the distinction by Cialdini and Goldstein (2004). This calls attention to the blurred boundaries between interpersonal and group processes in interpersonal influence situations. In fact, such situations in everyday life, especially in mediated encounters, are often embedded in larger intra- and intergroup structures; and even anonymous, zero-history encounters can evoke group processes (e.g., group identification, Reicher et al., 1995). Research of interpersonal influence thus needs to account for these additional processes, including but not limited to solidarity-building. For example, processes related to status differentiation (Ridgeway, 2001) might have been involved in the opposite effects of T–TE and T–T on the sources’ warmth and competence perceptions of the targets (as mentioned at the end of the Results section). These two perceptions are typically associated with status (Fiske et al., 2002). In advice interactions, the source is often situationally conceived to be high-status (e.g., expert, consultant, and coach) and the target assumes a low-status position, whereas in compliance-gaining interactions the status differentiation may be less relevant, if not reversed (e.g., salesperson and customer). Because a functional interaction ritual may bring not only solidarity but also hierarchy (Collins, 2004; Goffman, 1967), the functional T–TE (or antifunctional T–T) might have enhanced (or challenged) the typical status differentiation in the advice situations, causing the sources to perceive the targets as low-status and hence less warm and competent (or high-status and hence warmer and more competent).

Limitations and future directions

The current study has limitations. First and foremost, the richness of a conversation was inevitably lost in the quantification of conversational dynamics as sequential transitions, and in our simple Markov models of the average transition probabilities. For example, task and social–emotional acts can involve further subcategories and thus introduce an interpretive ambiguity when considering more nuanced alignment and misalignment for joint attention and shared emotion. We also did not study transition patterns beyond adjacent turns, even though such high-order transitions can be meaningful in influence conversations (see Wilson, 2002). In addition, we did not theorize changes of transition effects over time during a conversation. These issues may be addressed by incorporating larger datasets and using more advanced statistical models in future (e.g., latent transition analysis with random intercepts, Muthén & Asparouhov, 2022, although there are still questions about how to model causal effects of latent transitions on emergent, conversation/collective-level outcomes, i.e., the micro-to-macro links). Alternatively, research can use quantitative and qualitative methods together, especially given that our theoretical resources were almost all rooted in qualitative analyses (e.g., Goffman and Collins’ work, as well as the discourse research on interpersonal influence). One such direction is mixed-method cross-examinations between quantitative analysis and qualitative analysis. It may also be desirable to use simpler statistical models for general patterns in quantitative data (so as to avoid overfitting), and then use qualitative analyses to deal with the complexity and richness.

Second, while we identified consistent effects across two situations (advice giving and compliance gaining), it is still early to claim generalizability. Although we tried to improve ecological validity in the conversational tasks (e.g., assigning roles based on sleep quality, and apparently soliciting donations out of participants’ actual compensation), they were still role-playing exercises. Replications in real-world conversations are needed. Moreover, replications are needed in other contexts of advice-giving and compliance-gaining, and in other types of interpersonal influence situations (e.g., obtaining permissions, changing sociopolitical attitudes; see Dillard & Wilson, 2014). It is also important to study situations embedded in the mass-personal, mediated environments (O’Sullivan & Carr, 2018), in which interpersonal influence is not confined in isolated dyads but interweaving networks of personal and group relationships.

Finally, we only examined the most basic ingredients of interaction rituals (joint attention and shared emotion), leaving other aspects of Goffman (1967) and Collins’ (1981, 2004) insights untouched. For example, how conversationalists interactively create symbols of relationship and standards of morality (Collins, 2004) may shed light on the generation of conventional goals (Wilson, 2014) and the progression of message design logics (O’Keefe, 1988). We also ignored negative emotions, even though they can play context-dependent roles in interaction rituals and in real-world interpersonal influence situations. Therefore, further theorization is needed for understanding interpersonal influence through the interaction ritual perspective.

Conclusion

Interpersonal influence is a longstanding topic in communication research (e.g., the two-step flow model of communication). This article proposed both a conceptual and empirical approach to revisit the role that interaction and relational dynamics play in interpersonal influence situations. Interaction ritual theories, supplemented with quantifiable interaction patterns of task and social–emotional acts, provide a new lens to this issue; and we were able to demonstrate its interpretive and predictive power in two different interpersonal influence situations. We hope this article can instigate further communication research on the sociorelational aspects of interpersonal influence.
